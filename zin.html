03 — Spatial pavilion · 2025

ZIN

A pavilion designed for Terneuzen, bringing stillness into the coastal landscape.

Typology
Spatial pavilion / installation
Location
Terneuzen, Zeeland
Materials
Azobé timber, balsa, suspended linen, clay, rammed earth
Year
2025

ZIN is an open-air pavilion designed for Terneuzen in azobé timber. I imagined a still-water vessel at its centre, though that element was never realised.

Coastal context

I developed ZIN through site visits and conversations with local residents. That research helped me connect the pavilion to Zeeland’s long relationship with the sea.

Material and structure

A twelve-sided outer frame and an eight-sided inner structure support the suspended linen.

Light and movement

The North Sea wind moves the linen. Changing light casts shadows across the structure and the water.

Sensory pairings

I considered touch and movement, sight and hearing, warmth and smell. Each pairing offered a way to experience architecture beyond what we see.

Lower senses

Touch, Life, Movement, Balance

Middle senses

Smell, Taste, Sight, Warmth

Higher senses

Hearing, Word, Thought, “I”

Research and perception

My process began with architectural theory, particularly Peter Zumthor’s ideas on atmosphere and sensory experience. Research into Zeeland’s maritime history led me to explore how we perceive water beyond its practical function. I became interested in the twelve senses as different ways of experiencing and understanding our surroundings.

Sketches

Structural, material and sensory sketches tracing the pavilion’s development.

From possibilities to focus

At first, I imagined three pavilions, each exploring a different sensory pairing. I chose to focus on touch and movement because these were the ideas I had developed furthest. I realised I could not complete all three within the time I had.

Material and stillness

The azobé timber gives the pavilion a stable structure. Suspended linen responds to the wind and makes movement visible. Visits to churches shaped my thinking. I was drawn to their stillness and to the presence of holy water. A central vessel would have contrasted with the moving sea. ZIN became a way to explore how architecture can invite us to pause and be with ourselves.

For me, ZIN is a beginning rather than a conclusion. It opened up my interest in how architecture can reveal what often goes unnoticed and how we experience being part of our surroundings.
